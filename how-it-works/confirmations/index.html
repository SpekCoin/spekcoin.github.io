How it works

Confirmations

Three layers protect a transaction once it is in a block, and this page states each for what it is. There is no finality gadget, and nothing on this page pretends there is. (On this site settled describes a slot that has closed; a transaction is confirmed, and its depth is its number of confirmations.)

The 4-block fork window

Each block adds its work to its chain's chainwork, and the heavier chain wins. A block's work is the logarithm of how close its score came to the maximum; a null block's is 0. When a competing branch forks within 4 blocks of the tip, both branches are compared by chainwork once each holds at least two blocks above the fork point, and the loser is dropped. Waiting for both sides avoids switching back and forth on every block. The reorg is checked against a copy of the ledger before any live state changes, and rollback on this path is bounded at 5 blocks.

Forks deeper than the window, such as the two sides of a healed network partition, go to a full download that finds the true branch point, compares chainwork, and reorgs at any depth above the node's restoration pin.

Confirmations by attacker share

Draws are fixed per height, so an attacker can rewrite history only by out-weighing the honest chain over the same heights. The tables below give the probability that a transaction is ever reversible, and the confirmations needed for under one reversible slot per century, against an attacker holding share q of the draws who may choose its fork point in hindsight. Integrators choose their depth from the share they want to tolerate: for example 223 confirmations, about 1.9 hours, against an attacker holding a third.

The same numbers, in plain words

A confirmation is one more block built on top of yours, so it takes 30 seconds. q is the share of all the draws an attacker controls: 0.33 means one draw in three. The attacker is assumed to do the most damaging thing possible, looking back over the whole chain to pick the best moment to start a hidden branch.

How long to wait. Here "reversal" means the attacker's hidden branch overtakes the honest chain at some moment, even briefly, which is a harsher test than keeping control. The wait in the table is chosen so that, counting every one of the 1,051,200 slots in a year, the expected number of transactions ever reversed is less than one in a hundred years. The odds for any single transaction are far smaller than that, because the hundred years and a million slots a year are spread across all of them.

How the risk falls as you wait. The odds of a reversal drop very fast with each confirmation. For an attacker holding a third of the draws: after 6 confirmations (3 minutes) about 3 in 8 reversals succeed; after 30 (15 minutes) about 1 in 25; after 120 (an hour) about 1 in 37,000; after 240 (two hours) about 1 in 400 million. For an attacker holding 10%, 6 confirmations already leave about 1 chance in 320, and after 30 it is effectively never. Once an attacker holds close to half, no wait is safe for long. The table stops at 40% because past that share the attacker's private branch would run long enough to choose its own beacons, a case these numbers do not model.

Why chains weigh work, not raw scores

Chains used to weigh the sum of their raw scores. A null block scores 0 while a slot's best real score sits close to the maximum, so one null outweighed many slots of ordinary differences, and an attacker who withheld the bodies of slots where it held the five best headers could gain on the honest chain with a small share. Weighing each block by the logarithm of how close its score came to the maximum removes that: forcing nulls now helps only once an attacker holds a share of about 0.44 of the draws. The price is the longer waits in the table above. The details are in §6.1 of the white paper.

The full tables from the white paper

Numbers with an e in them are in scientific notation: the number after the minus sign says how many places the decimal point moves left, so each step down makes the chance about ten times smaller.

The model assumes the attacker's branch is scored against the same beacons as the honest chain, which holds for the first 1,441 blocks after its fork point. Beyond 1,441 blocks a private branch reads its own beacons, the case §17 treats with a break-even share of 0.44–0.46, so the tables stop at q = 0.40, whose depths (up to 679) stay inside 1,441. Forcing nulls on the honest chain does not help below about q = 0.44 (§6.1), so the model leaves it out. The figures come from a race model: the best of n independent uniform draws falls short of the maximum score by an exponentially distributed amount, a block's work is the logarithm of that shortfall (§6.1), so each slot moves the race between the two branches by ln(q/(1−q)) plus a logistic variable, and the attacker's branch wins a stretch of heights when its summed work exceeds the honest chain's. The probabilities are computed numerically (the law of the race's maximum on a fine lattice, saddlepoint tails for the sums), checked against Monte Carlo sampling, and the same method reproduces the closed forms of the earlier sum-of-scores rule exactly (reference 10).

Restoration pins

A running node never reorganises below its restoration pin: the newest checkpoint boundary buried at least one full interval below its tip. Checkpoint boundaries sit every 4,000 blocks (about 33 hours), so the pin trails the tip by 33–67 hours.

A partition longer than the pin is permanent

A pin moves with the node's own tip and needs no outside evidence. If the network splits for longer than the pin lag (33–67 hours), each side pins its own branch and refuses the other on reconnect as a checkpoint divergence. Nothing rejoins them automatically: operators have to choose a side. A shorter partition heals by itself through a deep reorganisation to the heavier side.

The checkpoint barrier

A node with no history of its own, such as a new node or one that was offline for days, trusts the checkpoints compiled into its release and pin files signed by a threshold of checkpoint signers. A fork whose branch point lies behind the node's restoration pin or a hard checkpoint is never resolved automatically, and the peer offering it is banned.

Why checkpoints are permanent

This is the nothing-at-stake problem of unbonded proof of stake: signing costs nothing and nothing can be slashed, so a key that held stake at a fork point can sign a branch from that point at any later time, even after its owner sold the coins. Hard checkpoints are therefore permanent weak subjectivity, not a launch measure, and are never retired. A node checks a pin file itself: it must name the network and genesis, carry signatures from a threshold of the checkpoint signers compiled into the binary, and may add to the compiled-in table but never contradict it.

No finality gadget

There is no finality gadget and no rollback limit shorter than the pin; the reasons are on Design choices. Coinbase outputs mature after 3,000 blocks, about 25 hours.
